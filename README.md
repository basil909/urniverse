# urniverse — You Are Here

An interactive, scroll-driven 3D journey from the Sun, past all eight planets, out to our place in the Milky Way.

Scroll (or use the arrow keys) and the camera flies from world to world. Each planet arrives centre-screen with its story, key numbers and a scale model, and the journey ends by zooming out until the whole Solar System is a single dot on a spiral arm: **you are here**.

## Features

- **An opening entrance.** As the loading screen lifts, the camera sweeps in from far above the planets' plane, past the Milky Way, into the opening view.
- **A generative soundtrack (off by default).** The small button beside the title turns on a quiet ambient score made live in the browser (Web Audio, no audio files): a soft chord that glides to a new one in every chapter, a breath of space wind and distant bells.
- **Cinematic camera journey.** Fourteen sections (intro, the Sun, eight planets, the Pale Blue Dot, the Milky Way, the Local Group, return), with the camera flying between them as you scroll.
- **Fly it yourself.** The small button (top right) pauses the journey and hands you NASA's Space Shuttle. Steer with the mouse (its offset from the faint centre ring sets how fast you turn), the arrow keys, a drag on touch screens, or a gamepad's left stick; W, a held click, the Thrust button or the right trigger speeds up and the speed holds, S slows, Q and E roll; the scroll wheel or C pulls the camera back until the Shuttle is a glowing speck against the planet. Every world has a quiet marker with its name and distance (an arrow at the screen's edge when it is off screen), its mark and tint telling its kind at a glance: the Sun rayed and gold, planets a white ring, asteroids a stone-coloured diamond, moons a silver crescent, spacecraft a mint tracking bracket, stars a pale blue glint; hovering one says what it is ("Ice giant · tipped on its side"), and arriving names it. Nothing is marked through the world you are near: what lies beyond it shows once it rises over the horizon. Moons, spacecraft, asteroids and the comet are marked only where you can see them, with no arrow at the edge. Close to a world, the rest of the sky goes quiet: worlds beyond its own neighbourhood keep only their mark, and their name and distance come back when you turn to look at them. Click one, press 1–8 (0 for the Sun), or open the Go to list (G), every world grouped by kind, to warp there: the ship climbs clear, crosses at warp (round the Sun if it is in the way) and arrives a few radii out on the sunlit side, the goal growing from a point to a world; S drops out of warp. Speed tops out at the speed of light (Mars is then minutes away, Neptune hours); Shift is a warp of up to 40 times light speed, and the HUD gives your true speed; Esc or Land returns you exactly where you were. The ship turns with a little weight and momentum, banks into turns, levels its wings to the horizon of the planet below, and eases its nose up rather than flying into the ground. It is drawn at its true size, 37 m against a 12,742 km Earth (some 340,000 ship-lengths across), in a render layer of its own; you can come down to the edge of space, 100 km above Earth, where the sky is black, the air a thin blue line on a curved horizon, and the clouds lie low over the ground; the HUD shows your altitude, and speed adapts to the nearest surface.
- **True size.** At the Sun and every planet but Earth, "See its true size" brings the camera in close on an Earth drawn exactly to scale beside the world ("This is Earth."), holds, then pulls slowly back while the world keeps growing into view ("And this is Jupiter."), ending on both in one frame with the figures: 11 Earths across, 1,321 Earths inside; 109 across and 1.3 million inside for the Sun. The Earth sits beside the world's sunlit edge at the same depth as its centre, so the comparison is not stretched by perspective; when it is a speck, a ring and its name mark it. Esc, a scroll or "Back to the story" returns to the chapter.
- **Living close-ups.** While you read, the camera slowly swings a little way round the planet and back, so moons, rings and stars shift with real depth.
- **Text that waits for the camera.** Each chapter's text appears only once the camera has arrived, and stays pinned while you read.
- **Real positions and motion.** Planets sit where they actually are on the visitor's date, on correctly oriented and tilted orbits (JPL orbital elements, Kepler's laws). In the wide views time runs at a few days per second so you can watch the orbits; Venus and Uranus spin backwards, as they really do.
- **A real sky.** The Milky Way panorama surrounds the scene, turned to its true ~60° angle to the planets' plane, with every star visible to the naked eye (the 9,096 stars of the Yale Bright Star Catalogue) at its true position, brightness and colour (the very brightest with soft diffraction spikes), a luminous Milky Way band with dark dust rifts, golden towards the galactic centre, and real deep-sky objects at their true positions and true apparent sizes: the Orion, Carina, Lagoon, Eagle, Rosette and North America nebulae, the Andromeda Galaxy and the Magellanic Clouds, each as bright as it is to the eye under a dark sky (the telescope-only ones faint), in long-exposure colours; the band's dark rifts (the Great Rift, the Coalsack) and bright star clouds (Sagittarius, Scutum, Cygnus, Carina) are where they really are; constellations and a few star names show faintly in the opening view.
- **Real light and shadow.** Planets spin about their real IAU poles (true tilts and seasons). Saturn's rings and the planet shadow each other, moons cast shadows on their planets, and the Sun has granulation, limb darkening and a soft corona.
- **Real planet surfaces.** Photographic texture maps for every planet, Saturn's rings with real gaps, Earth with clouds, city lights and its Moon. Each planet has a hand-written shader fallback.
- **Sharp close-ups.** Once the camera arrives at a planet, 4K maps of it are decoded off the main thread and swapped in, one upload at a time; they are released when you move on, so only one planet's sharp maps are ever in memory. Phones keep the lighter 2K maps.
- **Drawn to scale.** The panel shows each world beside Earth (Earth beside the Moon) to scale, and the Solar System from above with where it and Earth are today.
- **Deep dives.** "More about" opens a panel, in four tabs (Overview, Right now, Story, Exploration) under a bar that stays at hand, for the Sun, each planet, the Pale Blue Dot, the Milky Way and the Local Group: key figures (with each planet's air and its distance from the Sun), what makes it unusual, its best-known moons, and a timeline of the missions or discoveries. "Right now" works out where the world is today (its distance from Earth and from the Sun, and how long its light takes to reach us; for the Pale Blue Dot, Voyager 1's distance and the day-long signal), and counts how far it has moved since you opened the page. The Pale Blue Dot has a ladder of distances out to the nearest star, the Milky Way a sketch of the galaxy from above with the Sun marked. A bar keeps the name and close at hand as you read, and Previous / Next at the foot fly on to the neighbouring chapter and open its panel when you arrive.
- **Links to every chapter.** Each chapter has its own address (…/#saturn, …/#milky-way); opening one goes straight there once the loading screen lifts, the address follows the journey, the browser's back and forward step between chapters, and the panel has a "Link" button to copy it.
- **You, there.** In each world's panel, type your weight and age to see what you would weigh there and how many of its years old you would be (any unit; nothing is stored or sent).
- **In your sky tonight.** For each planet, when it is up while the sky is dark where you are (which way to look, how high it gets), when it rises and sets, which constellation it is in and how bright it is; sunrise and sunset in the Sun's panel, the galaxy's centre in the Milky Way's, Andromeda in the Local Group's. Worked out in the browser from the same orbital elements, for a place guessed from your time zone, or your own with "Use my location" (the browser asks; it never leaves the page).
- **More to tap.** Points of interest on every planet (Mercury's Caloris Basin and Hokusai, Venus's Maxwell Montes and Aphrodite Terra beneath the clouds, Jupiter's polar cyclones, Saturn's hexagon and equatorial jet, Uranus's polar haze, Neptune's methane clouds and warm south pole), and the moons named as you arrive (Phobos and Deimos, the four Galilean moons, Titan), each with its story on a tap.
- **Feel the distance.** Each hop between neighbouring worlds shows, as you fly it, how far apart they really are today and how long light takes to cross (Earth to Mars: some minutes; Saturn to Uranus: hours).
- **Where the spacecraft are.** The Pale Blue Dot's panel maps Voyager 1, Voyager 2 and New Horizons in their true directions, at today's distances (squeezed to fit).
- **The Local Group.** One more step out: the Milky Way and its neighbours at their true distances and directions on the same scale, Andromeda 2.5 million light-years away (25 Milky Ways), Triangulum beyond it, the Magellanic Clouds beside us.
- **The time bar.** Along the foot of the journey (the clock button on a phone): the date and time the scene is showing, and how it runs: Cinematic (the planets drifting gently so their motion shows), Live (everything on the real clock, now), or an hour, a day or a week a second; "Back to now" when the scene has drifted from today.
- **Key words.** In each chapter's sentence its key fact is set in white (for Earth, "the reference point"), so the sentence reads at a glance.
- **Where we have landed.** Diamonds mark the landing and impact sites of missions, each with its date: Perseverance, Curiosity, Opportunity, Viking 1 and Zhurong on Mars, Venera 13 on Venus, MESSENGER on Mercury.
- **Search everything.** Ctrl K (or /, or the search button) opens a command palette: every chapter, every panel tab ("mars miss" opens Mars's Exploration), every place the Shuttle can fly to (Enter takes off and warps there), and the actions (Live time, sound, the link, back to the start). Arrow keys to choose, Enter to go.
- **A live figure from the start.** Under the opening title, how far Earth has carried you round the Sun since you arrived, ticking at 29.78 km/s.
- **Figures as tiles.** A panel's At a glance figures sit in a grid of tiles with the first (the diameter, say) as a large headline tile; the drawings are numbered Fig. 01, Fig. 02.
- **Pick a world.** In the opening view and at the end, point at any world for a ring and its name; a click (or Enter) flies the journey to its chapter. Each has a finger-sized target, so even Mercury is easy to hit.
- **Compare.** Each planet's panel has a Compare tab: pick any other planet to see both drawn to scale, a line on how they differ (how many would fit inside, what you would weigh), and their diameter, gravity, temperature, spin, year, distance and moons side by side, the larger of each picked out.
- **A sense of scale.** Every planet chapter shrinks the Sun to a 2 m door and tells you how big that planet would be and how far away, with a track showing true relative distances.
- **A Pale Blue Dot finale.** From beyond Neptune the camera looks back through a long lens at Earth, a single speck beside the Sun's glare, as Voyager 1 saw it in 1990. Then a long pull-back: the lens opens, the Solar System collapses to a point, and a "You are here" marker appears on a spiral arm of the Milky Way.
- **Quiet, editorial interface.** Two typefaces, a slim chapter rail and a hairline progress bar; the planet is always the hero.
- **Relief and living detail.** Mercury and Mars are shaded from real elevation maps, so craters and volcanoes catch the light; Jupiter's and Saturn's cloud bands flow and the Great Red Spot turns; the Sun has prominences; Jupiter's large moons show their real surfaces; the asteroid belt (with Jupiter's Trojans) and the Kuiper belt orbit at their true rates; a comet with ion and dust tails rounds the Sun during the opening view; the giants are flattened by their spin, as they really are (Saturn 10% wider at the equator than pole to pole, Jupiter 6.5%, Uranus and Neptune about 2%), their air and the light round Saturn's rim flattened with them; the Milky Way glows with a bright core, spiral arms and dust lanes.
- **See inside.** From a planet's "More about" panel, "See inside" swaps the planet for a cut-open model: a quarter wedge removed, its layers (core, mantle, crust, or the giants' hydrogen and ice) painted on the cut faces and numbered to a key in the panel.
- **Backlit light.** In the backlit Venus, Earth and Saturn shots, soft rays fan out from the planet's sunlit rim.
- **Hands-on planets.** Drag to rotate the planet in focus; small markers on real features (Olympus Mons, the Great Red Spot, the Himalayas and more) reveal a short note on hover.
- **Works on phones.** On portrait screens the planet moves to the top half and the text to the bottom.
- **Accessible.** All chapter text is available to screen readers, there is keyboard navigation, and the OS "reduce motion" setting turns off smooth scrolling and camera drift.
- **Fast first load.** The first load needs only the 2K WebP textures (about 2 MB), self-hosted fonts and a loading screen that tracks real progress.

## What is real, and what is simplified

**Real**
- Planet positions and orbits for the simulated date (JPL orbital elements), their spin axes and tilts (IAU), relative rotation rates and directions.
- Planet sizes relative to each other (Jupiter is 11 Earths wide, Saturn 9).
- Earth's orientation: it is turned by sidereal time, so the day and night sides (and the city lights) match the simulated moment.
- The Moon's position and phase (a short lunar series, good to about a degree), with its near side always facing Earth.
- Jupiter's four large moons at their real orbital longitudes (Lieske's E5 mean longitudes); every moon at its real period on the same clock.
- The bright stars (positions and colours), the Milky Way's angle across the sky, Neptune in true colour.
- Figures and facts in the text and panels (NASA fact sheets; dated where they change).

**Simplified**
- In the journey, distances are compressed so everything fits on screen (in flight they are true: see below); the Sun is drawn much smaller than its true 109 Earth-widths; moons are enlarged and drawn closer to their planets. The text gives the true scale model.
- By default the motion is calm: the planets drift along their real orbits from today's positions (faster in the wide views, slowly in close-ups), while spins and moons turn at a gentle pace of their own. The **Real motion** button (top right) puts everything on one clock from this moment, at 1 hour, 1 day or 1 week per second, so orbits, Earth's day and night, the Moon's phases and the moons all keep true time together; the clock is shown beside it.
- The comet is illustrative, not a real comet. Saturn's, Mars's and Uranus's moons have real periods but not real positions. The Milky Way model is a sketch of a barred spiral after today's maps (a bar about 16,000 light-years each way; four arms at a pitch of about 12.5°, the two major ones leaving the bar's ends; the Sun 26,000 light-years out in the Orion Spur, between the Sagittarius and Perseus arms; old orange stars in the bulge, bar and halo, young blue ones in the arms), not a map of the real arms star by star, shown only in the finale (from inside the galaxy it can never appear as a spiral in our sky). 
- Weather details (Jupiter's flowing bands, Neptune's dark spot, Mars's dust storm) are artistic, based on what spacecraft have seen. They move on the simulation's clock at about the real winds' pace: still when time is held, flowing when it runs fast.
- In flight, everything is at true scale: the 37 m Shuttle, the planets' sizes, their distances (1 AU is 150 million km; Neptune is 4.5 billion km out) and the Sun, 109 Earths wide and half a degree across seen from Earth. The Solar System holds still while you fly (Earth alone moves 30 km a second and turns 465 m a second at the equator), so you can stand on it. The asteroid belt, Jupiter's Trojans and the Kuiper belt stand at their true distances, far apart as they really are (shown as a faint dust of points); the four largest asteroids, Ceres, Vesta, Pallas and Hygiea, are at their real positions (JPL orbits) and true sizes, shaped from noise since no maps of them are bundled. The comet, which is illustrative, is hidden in flight.
- The asteroid belt and Jupiter's Trojans in flight: some 12,000 asteroids between Mars and Jupiter (thinned at the Kirkwood gaps) and two swarms of 2,000 sharing Jupiter's orbit 60 degrees ahead of it and behind, on their real Kepler orbits. Flying through the belt or a swarm, rocks from house-sized to a couple of km tumble past the ship, drawn far denser than they are (real asteroids are around a million km apart, and you could cross the belt seeing nothing; a note says so). The two largest Trojans can be visited, on their JPL orbits at their true sizes: 624 Hektor, two lobes in contact, 225 km end to end, ahead of Jupiter; and 617 Patroclus, behind it, really a pair (Patroclus and Menoetius, 680 km apart, circling each other every 4.3 days).
- Warp anywhere: Shift alone punches it, even skimming a planet. The speed multiplies by orders of magnitude, building as you leave a world and easing down as you close on one (it follows the distance to the nearest world, more loosely than plain flight, up to 40 times light speed in open space), with the warp's streaks and sound past a third of light speed; letting go drops out of warp. At those speeds one frame can cross a planet, so each step is checked against every world and the Sun, and the ship drops out just short of one in its path.
- Focus: flying, everything far away is drawn lightly. Planets and moons only a few pixels across are drawn as light spheres (the same at that size, a fraction of the work), anything detailed smaller than half a pixel is not drawn, and near a world the belts and a distant comet step back, so the frame goes to the world you are at (the automatic quality keeps its sky, clouds and auroras at full detail for longer).
- Warps arrive close (about 1.6 radii out) so a planet overfills the view behind the tiny ship, and a note gives its size in Shuttles: Earth is 342,000 Shuttles across, Jupiter 3.8 million.
- A real comet: 2P/Encke, on its true orbit (JPL Small-Body Database elements), in November 2026 some 1.7 AU from the Sun on its way in to perihelion in February 2027. Its dark 4.8 km nucleus, a coma tens of thousands of km across whose glow is worked out exactly along each line of sight (gas thinning as the square of the distance), so you can fly into it, and two tails millions of km long: the blue ion tail streaming straight away from the Sun, the yellow-white dust tail curving back along the orbit. All of it grows as the comet nears the Sun. It has its own marker and a place in the Go to list.
- Auroras at Earth, Jupiter and Saturn, in 3D up close: curtains on an oval round each magnetic pole (nearer the pole on the day side), thin folded sheets streaked with vertical rays and a sharp lower edge, seen on the night side. Earth's glow green from about 100 km (oxygen), red higher up, purple at the bottom (nitrogen); the giants' glow in hydrogen's pinks and violets, hundreds of km up. From afar the painted aurora on Earth's night side remains.
- Shuttle missions from the Go to list. Launch from Kennedy: the full stack (orbiter, orange external tank and the two white solid rocket boosters, from NASA's model) stands on Launch Complex 39A and flies the Shuttle's own ascent, heading north-east as for the Space Station: watched from the ground at first, then from behind; the roll program, Max Q, the boosters falling away at 2:04 with their great plumes gone, main engine cut-off at 8:30, 113 km up at 7.7 km/s, the tank falling away, and into orbit (the mission clock shows the real T+ time, running 2.5 times faster). Fly home to Kennedy: re-entry from 1,500 km out and 80 km up at 6.5 km/s, nose held 40 degrees up so the belly's tiles take the heat, down the real profile of height and speed, lined up on the Shuttle Landing Facility's runway 15; about 6 km out the landing takes over with the Shuttle's real approach: 155 m/s down a 19 degree glide aimed 2.3 km short of the runway, the pull-up some 600 m up onto the shallow 1.5 degree inner glide, touchdown on the centreline about 760 m past the threshold at about 360 km/h, and a long roll-out (as with the drag chute) stopping past the runway's middle.
- Every world in its true pose, and their shadows on one another. Each planet turns to the face it really shows at that moment (Earth by sidereal time, the others by the IAU's rotation models: Mars with Olympus Mons where it really is now), in the journey as in flight, and the large moons keep the right face to their planet. The Moon's place comes from the main terms of the ELP-2000 lunar theory (Meeus), good to well under an arcminute, so eclipses happen on their real dates: the Moon's shadow crosses Earth (a dark core in a penumbra thousands of km across, on the ground, the sea and the clouds; 2 August 2027 over Egypt at 10:07 UT, as predicted), the Moon turns copper in Earth's shadow (3 March 2026), and a moon behind its planet goes dark (Io every 42 hours). Run time with T to watch them.
- The Shuttle's moving parts: the landing gear comes down on final approach (about 90 m up, as on the real one) and up after take-off; the drag chute streams out at touchdown and is let go at about 110 km/h; the split rudder opens as a speed brake on the approach and wide on the roll-out (and whenever you slow in air); the elevons work with pitch and roll in air; in space, the attitude thrusters in the nose and tail pods puff as a turn starts and stops; the main engines gimbal a few degrees with the turn. It stands on its wheels, the belly about 2 m up.
- Earth's mountains and storms from orbit: the globe's elevation (AWS Terrain Tiles, zoom 3, reprojected to latitude and longitude) is marched towards the Sun near the day/night line, so the Himalayas, the Andes and the Rockies cast their long shadows when the light is low; the thickest cloud stands tallest (storm anvils reach 15 km), its tops lit like domes and throwing shadows across the lower cloud at sunrise and sunset.
- Yesterday's real weather on Earth: near Earth, NASA's daily true-colour imagery (GIBS: VIIRS on NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 and MODIS on Aqua, the previous UTC day) is stitched into a global map and turned into Earth's clouds (bright and colourless is cloud), so the hurricanes, fronts and cloud streets are the real ones, where they really were. Snow is bright and colourless too: NASA's daily snow map from the same VIIRS cameras (NDSI snow cover, made with a proper cloud mask) says where the white was snow, so it is taken out of the clouds and laid on the ground instead: yesterday's real snow on the Rockies, the Andes, the Himalaya and the northern plains, over maps that are otherwise snow-free summer. Sea ice still reads as cloud. Round where you are looking, the same day's imagery is fetched again much finer and laid over the global map (2 km a pixel from orbit, 1 km below 150 km, 500 m flying low; desktop), so from the Space Station's height the cumulus fields, cloud streets and clear gaps are sharp, and flying low the 3D clouds, their shadows and the mist follow the real clouds' shapes. Gaps between the satellites' passes and the polar night are filled from the usual cloud map. The same clouds cast the shadows, form the 3D clouds flying low and the mist you fly through. Credited on screen with the date.
- The Space Station, Hubble and Webb are drawn at their true sizes (109 m, 13.2 m, 21 m) against the 37 m Shuttle; warps now arrive alongside them, and a note gives their size in Shuttle lengths.
- Earth from orbit as photographed: clouds with firm edges, lit by the Sun itself (bright white by day, warm near the day/night line), not a grey veil; and the air's haze eases from space with its glow, so deserts stay tan, forests green and oceans deep blue.
- Living oceans: flying low, Earth's sea moves: six trains of waves from a long swell to wind chop, travelling at their true deep-water speeds, each fading once finer than a pixel and roughening the water instead, so the Sun's glint is a sharp mirror up close and a wide glitter path far off; the sky is mirrored in it, more so at a low angle. Titan's seas of liquid methane round its north pole (dark in Cassini's mosaic) are drawn as near-still mirrors under its orange sky.
- Clouds in 3D, flying low over Earth: below about 60 km the flat cloud deck gives way to cloud you fly over, between and through, from about 1.5 km up to 9 km where it is thick (low cumulus where it is broken), its cover read from the same cloud map and its billows from 3D noise, drifting with the weather. Each cloud is lit through itself (bright sunlit tops, darker bases, orange at sunset) and casts its shadow on the ground and sea below.
- The giants close up: finer than any map (NASA's best global map of Jupiter shows nothing under ~120 km, and there is none of Saturn), their clouds are given generated detail that follows the real bands: turbulence strongest where two bands shear past each other, small curls a few hundred km across, and fine filaments drawn out along the jets, all moving with the clouds; Jupiter roiling, Saturn softer, the ice giants gentle. The map is read at its own sharpness however it is swirled, and the bright zones are kept just below the glow so a giant filling the view does not wash out.
- Rings on all four giants: Saturn's, and now Jupiter's faint dusty rings (halo, main ring, two gossamer rings), Uranus's nine narrow dark rings and its faint outer two, and Neptune's five at their true distances, Adams gathered into its four bright arcs. They are lit by the Sun, dimmer seen from their unlit face, crossed by the planet's shadow, and the dusty ones shine out when lit from behind.
- The same real-scattering air on Venus (a thick yellowed haze above its clouds) and the giants (tan hazes over Jupiter and Saturn, a clear blue limb on Uranus and Neptune). Neptune is shown in its true pale azure (Irwin et al. 2024): the familiar deep blue came from contrast-enhanced Voyager images.
- A calm readout: one line at the bottom, what is happening in small capitals, the figures in steady digits, and the key that changes it (W goes round, L to land) shown for a few seconds before it fades. The data credits fold into a faint Sources, shown in full for a few seconds when new data appears and on hover or tap. On touch screens the guide speaks of dragging and tapping, Thrust sits clear of the buttons and a Land button appears near a solid world. Near the Space Station or Hubble, Earth stays the focus (far worlds quiet).
- Speed: F shows a quiet frame meter (frames a second, the time a frame takes and its worst, the graphics card's own time where the browser reports it, draw calls, triangles, the quality step; also ?perf in the address). The effects marched along each line of sight (the air round the planets, the 3D clouds, the auroras) are drawn at half resolution in a pass of their own and laid over the frame, each still hidden by whatever stands in front of it; shift+F switches them to full size to compare. In the journey, a planet's sharp 4K maps go up to the graphics card in 1024 x 512 tiles, one a frame, and are let go one at a time when you move on (each used to cost a 30-80 ms jolt as the camera settled on the planet; Earth's four, some 200 ms of rough frames). Each sharp map is fetched, decoded and cut into its tiles in a background worker, so its arrival costs the page nothing (it held that frame 40-90 ms), and Earth's mountain-shadow map is built in one too, kept as one byte a pixel (it held a frame 100 ms, a second after leaving Earth). The shaders are compiled ahead for the effects' buffers they are drawn into: compiled for the screen, the auroras and clouds, the Galaxy and its stars were each built again when first seen, 120-230 ms at Earth and at the Milky Way on a first visit. The planets' surface shader is compiled once per planet, each with only its own surface, and works out the procedural stand-in surface only where no map has arrived: as one program for all seven, Chrome and Edge on Windows (whose WebGL runs on Direct3D) took some 21 s to compile it on a first visit, and the page froze if a planet was drawn before it was done (up to 19 s in testing). The giants' programs now take about 5 s each, side by side, and whatever is still compiling when the loading screen lifts stays out of the picture until its shader is ready, instead of the page waiting for it. The Shuttle model is put together a few milliseconds at a time (in one go it held the journey for a quarter of a second, some ten seconds after the page opened). On a first visit no frame of the journey now takes more than about 45 ms. Yesterday's weather maps and the sharp cloud patch are worked out in a background worker (they used to stall the flight for most of a second on reaching Earth), and streamed ground imagery and elevation go up to the graphics card tile by tile instead of a whole 16 MB picture each time. At the start, every map is decoded once, off the main thread, and shared by everything that uses it (some were decoded two or three times), then handed to the graphics card about one large map a frame; the shaders compile side by side while the loading screen is up; the Shuttle model waits until you fly or the page is idle, and each world's landing maps until a ship heads for it. The main thread is now blocked for about a quarter of a second at the start instead of a full second. The textures, models and star data are cached by the browser for a week (next.config.ts). Grey maps sit on the graphics card in the channels their shaders read rather than four (the sea mask and the height and bump maps in one, the cloud maps in two): the same pixels in a quarter or half of the memory, about 50 MB less. The Hubble and Perseverance models no longer carry a PNG or JPEG copy of every texture beside the WebP one that is used (1.7 MB to 0.2 MB, 4.9 MB to 1.9 MB), and a model of many small parts has the parts that share a material merged in place as it loads, a few milliseconds at a time (Webb: 1,657 draw calls to 169; Perseverance: 504 to 132; the same triangles). The textures stay WebP: the GPU formats that keep full quality (KTX2 UASTC) would make each file several times larger to download.
- Clear controls: H (or the Controls button) shows every control by group; the bottom-left row is Exit, Go to, Controls. Steering pauses while the pointer is over a marker or a button, so reaching to click one no longer turns the ship, and when it leaves the window.
- Real air on Earth and Mars: sunlight scattered once on its way through the air, worked out along every line of sight (Rayleigh scattering by the gas, Mie by haze and dust). Earth's sky is blue and its sunsets orange-red; Mars's sky is the butterscotch of its dust, and round the setting Sun it turns blue, because the dust throws blue light further forward. The sky from the ground, the sunset and the thin blue edge seen from orbit are one calculation. From far off the air is drawn thicker (its colour unchanged) so that it shows.
- Landing (Mercury, Earth, the Moon, Mars and the large moons): press L anywhere within a few radii of a solid world and the Shuttle lands itself, as the real one did: it comes down (glowing through the air where there is air), flies a steep glide, flares a few tens of metres up, touches down at about 300 km/h and rolls out to a stop; W before touchdown goes round again, L again calls it off. Holding W takes off: a roll along the ground, rotation at about 300 km/h, a climb, and the controls are yours again 300 m up. The ground's large shapes are real (Mars's and Mercury's elevation maps, Earth's coastlines, with the sea flat), its colours the maps and NASA's streamed imagery; relief below a few km is generated. The sky lights up as you come down into the air (blue on Earth, butterscotch on Mars, black on airless Mercury). Venus (hidden under its clouds) and the giants (no surface) cannot be landed on.
- Coming down through air (Earth, Mars, Titan) is a real entry: the Shuttle meets the air at the speed ships do (7.8 km/s 120 km over Earth, about 5.6 km/s at Mars, 6 km/s at Titan) and slows as it thickens, in 20 to 30 seconds rather than half an hour. The readout says Atmospheric entry, then Peak heating, with the hull temperature (up to about 1,650 °C on Earth); the plasma sheath builds on the nose, the hot air's glow creeps in from the screen's edges, the camera shakes, and at the hottest a Radio blackout tag shows. Touchdown jolts the camera and throws up dust at the main gear (on Earth, tyre smoke); the wheels throw rooster tails on the roll-out and the engines blast the ground on the take-off roll. With no air the dust flies in clean arcs and falls straight back; in air it billows and hangs.
- Landed, a card says where you are: the region or feature ("Mare Crisium", or how far and which way from the nearest), the coordinates and the nearest landing site ("InSight landed 886 km to the south-west"); the local solar time and where the Sun is; what hangs in the sky (Earth from Mars and the Moon, Jupiter from its moons, Mars from Phobos), worked out from the scene; your weight there, the temperature, the air, how long a day lasts and the sky's colour, and one thing worth knowing. Your landings are counted in this browser ("Landing 4 · your first on Mars"). From the card: step outside, plant a flag, watch a whole day go by (sunrise to sunrise in about 40 seconds, the Shuttle carried with the turning world), save a postcard (the view with the place, world, coordinates and date along its foot) or take off.
- Sharper worlds and a truer Earth: flying low over Mars, the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter's Context Camera mosaic (CTX beta01, Murray Lab, Caltech; served by Esri) streams in down to about 5 m a pixel, laid over the Viking colour as light and shade (each sharp tile divided by a copy four times coarser, so the mosaic's strip-to-strip brightness cancels and the craters, dunes and channels remain). Earth's real elevation now reaches the graphics card where it should (three.js reads a texture's partial updates as if it had four values a pixel; the elevation's two-value tiles went up to the wrong places, so the ground was drawn flat where the ship stood on real heights). The cloud layer lifts over high ground (its base at least 1.2 km above the ground below you), so a landing in the Andes is not buried in fog. Yesterday's real clouds are read more carefully (scattered cumulus averaged into a 16 km pixel, and the Sun's glint off the sea, no longer count as solid cloud), and part cover is drawn as broken cloud with ground between, not a sheet. Pressing L over the sea, the Shuttle makes for the nearest coast (up to 3,000 km off: across above the air, then in through it at up to orbital speed, glowing) and lands some 40 km inland; close in, Earth's ground takes its cue from the imagery: grass in clumps and tufts where it is green, rippled sand and gravel where it is bare desert.
- The ground up close: below what the maps and generated relief hold, each pixel adds the ground at your feet, from about 30 m down to a centimetre, each scale appearing once it spans a few pixels: bumps and hollows that tilt the light (rougher in places, smoother in others); on airless worlds small craters from a metre to a hundred, the young ones with raised rims and bright ejecta, filling with shadow from the sunward rim when the Sun is low; gravel; on Mars soft-edged patches of dark sand laid in ripples a metre and a half apart, and of bright dust, with craters worn and dust-filled; the ground's colour close in as rovers see it (browner than from orbit). Dusty airless ground takes light as regolith does: nearly as bright at a slant as face on, and brightening straight away from the Sun (the glow round your own shadow). The patterns are kept in metres from an anchor that steps in whole 2,048 m strides, so nothing shifts as you move. Mars's sky is the rovers' pale butterscotch.
- On foot: the view drops to a person's eye height beside the Shuttle. W and S walk (Shift hurries), A and D turn, a drag looks around, Space jumps: off the ground at 3.1 m/s under the world's own gravity (weakening with height on the small moons), 1.3 m on Mars, 3 m and four seconds on the Moon, hundreds of metres and many minutes on Phobos and Deimos (shown faster, and said so). Footprints stay in the dust behind you; Esc goes back aboard. On touch screens Thrust becomes Walk, with a Jump button beside it. The near ground gets rocks: boulders out to 300 m and pebbles at your feet, the same each time at a given place, sharp on the Moon and Mars, rounded on Titan as Huygens found them (none on Earth, which could be sea or a runway). The flag is no country's: a world and its orbit on the night sky; on an airless world it is held out by a rod along its top, as Apollo's were, and in air it flies.
- Earth, flying low: the ground is real. Its elevation streams from AWS Open Data Terrain Tiles (mostly NASA SRTM; mountains, valleys and coasts as they are) and its imagery from Sentinel-2 cloudless 2016 at about 10 m a pixel; a little generated roughness is added below their resolution. Yesterday's snow lies on it as snow does: the satellites' share of white ground in each 16 km sets a snow line, so the high ground goes white first, steep rock and dark ground show through and its edges break into drifts. Each point of the ground mesh takes the elevation averaged over the patch it stands for, so the far, coarse rings of the mesh do not shimmer or spike as you fly.
- In flight: diving fast into a world's air wraps the ship in re-entry plasma (orange at the hull, violet at the edge, with a glowing wake and a shaking camera), driven by the air's density and the speed. The ship goes dark in a planet's shadow and is lit red through the air at orbital sunrise and sunset, when the Sun breaks over the limb in a burst of rays; low over the ground, the Shuttle casts its shadow. V cycles the camera (chase, fly-by, orbit); P is photo mode, with no HUD, and K saves the view as a picture.
- Also in flight: sound (with the sound switch on: engines, wind only where there is air, the roar and crackle of re-entry, warp's rising whine, touchdown); streaks of air racing past low down, and buffeting; Earth's cities lit on the night side from the ground; Earth's cloud deck (about 10 km up) to fly through, in mist; the Moon at its true size and distance, to land on (LRO elevation and imagery); the giants and Venus, to sink into: their haze darkens with depth, the pressure is shown (from the temperature's rise with depth) until it turns you back. The large moons stand at their true sizes and distances in flight, at their real positions, and can be landed on: Io, Europa (with its reddish cracks, generated), Ganymede, Callisto, Titan (under its orange haze), Phobos and Deimos (drawn round; generated relief). Near Saturn's ring plane, chunks of ice from pebbles to boulders tumble past, as dense as the ring map says (none in the Cassini Division), and the rings become the horizon. O puts the Shuttle into a circular orbit at its height, at the true orbital speed (93 minutes round Earth at the Space Station's height); T runs time (held still, real time, ×60, ×600, ×3,600) so worlds turn and moons move, and a ship standing on a world is carried with it. Flying low, real features are named as they come into view (Olympus Mons, Tycho, the Matterhorn, Gale Crater...). Fly to the Sun: granules a thousand km across resolve as you come in, sunspots show their umbra and penumbra, prominences rise off the limb, and the hull temperature climbs until the heat holds you back. Leave the Solar System: out past about 2,000 AU the sky is handed to 11,398 stars at their true 3D places (HYG database), so the constellations shift as you travel; warp to twenty named stars (Alpha Centauri, Sirius, Betelgeuse...), each drawn at its size and colour, and look back at the Sun as a bright star; crossing the heliopause (about 120 AU) is marked. Visit spacecraft at their true sizes (NASA models): the Space Station and Hubble on their real orbits (not their live places along them), Webb near L2, Voyager 1 (a light-day out) and 2 in their real directions and distances, Perseverance in Jezero crater. Close to Io, Europa, Ganymede and Titan, NASA's Galileo/Voyager and Cassini mosaics stream in (NASA Trek). A first flight is guided in three steps; each planet's chapter has a Fly there button; the picture quality steps down by itself if frames run long. Speed scales with the distance to the nearest world, so the planets are seconds apart.
- In flight, low over Earth, Mars or Mercury, the ground beneath you is NASA's own imagery, streamed tile by tile while you fly (Blue Marble and the VIIRS city lights at about 500 m a pixel, Viking's Mars mosaic, MESSENGER's Mercury basemap); Mars and Mercury are colour-matched to the maps. Detail finer than any data, the eddies on the giants and the finest ringlets are generated texture. Lightning in Earth's night-side clouds and the airglow line on its night limb are real phenomena, drawn illustratively.

### How the scroll works

The page is a tall empty scroll track; nothing visible scrolls. Scroll position becomes a single `progress` number (section index plus how far into it you are), which drives everything:

- **The camera** (`UniverseCanvas.tsx`) has a rest pose per section and eases between them during the first 18% of each section.
- **The text** (`Journey.tsx`) is shown only between 16% and 93% of a section, after the camera has arrived and before it leaves.
- **Planets** follow their orbits as progress advances; non-focused planets shrink to dots so they never crowd a close-up.

To add or remove a chapter, edit the `JOURNEY` array in `Journey.tsx` and add a matching camera pose in `getRestPose` in `UniverseCanvas.tsx`. Section counts, the chapter rail and the progress bar update automatically.
